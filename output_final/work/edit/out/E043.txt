### FR125:82-275
An order to the prophet, peace be upon him: the shortest and most direct way of giving an order to get a message across. Synonyms would have to be linked to other words. It's like saying "tell": well, tell whom? You can't just say "tell". However, you can give the command to say.

We have God referring to those that believe in him as disbelievers, negating their claim to faith. He labels them as kafaroon, not kafara or any other synonym, for the purpose of the upcoming rhyme in the verse. The translation provided by David says, "Say, O ye that reject Faith." This is not an accurate translation. In fact, a 6th Century expert in the field of eloquence says directly: that is avoided in the verse. So it doesn't say, "Say, oh ye that reject Faith," but rather it says, "Say, [O ye] disbelievers." That would be a closer translation.

So the question is: why does the actual verse say "disbelievers" instead of something like "that reject faith"? Well, it's because the disbelief of disbeliever is greater in affirmation than one that is rejecting faith, which seems temporary. For example, there's a difference between one who steals and a thief: someone that stole once, or someone that stole at one point in their life, isn't the same as someone who practices it regularly, to the extent that they're referred to as a thief. But there's a greater reason why this word was chosen, and it's going to be connected to verse number three, which I'll get to later.

To summarize, the verse is an order from the Almighty to his Prophet to relay a message to disbelievers that reject him.

La abudu: the translation provided by David says, "I worship not that which ye worship." Not entirely correct: when the term [la] is connected to a verb in the present tense, it also refers to the future. So the correct translation is: "I will not worship that which ye worship, nor are you worshipers of that which I worship." Notice how it says "that which ye worship": the term ma here is often used to encompass all of which you worship, without specifying which idol or which idols, in order to encompass all their false deities: idols, the angels, etc., anything they worship.

Then we have "nor are you worshipers of that which I worship." Here the verse refers to them as abedoon, negating that they are worshipers of what he worships, despite being believers in Allah; however, their polytheism has nullified their worship of him. Also, it negated them as worshipers of Allah as a noun and not as a verb, in the same way that it referred to them as disbelievers early on in verse number one, instead of those that simply commit disbelief.

The verse ends again with [ma]: "and you are not worshipers of what I worship," using the same ma, which is usually used as "what" or "which", same as the previous verse, in order to mirror it, but now it means "who", or men in Arabic. The usage, of course, is a correct one linguistically.

To summarize, the Almighty is telling his Prophet to tell the pagans that His prophets will not worship their idols, and that they will not worship Him. Allah isn't talking to them directly, but he is telling his Messenger to tell them; otherwise it would have said, "Oh disbelievers, my Prophet worships me, and you are not worshiping me." Now compare it to this: "Say, O ye disbelievers, I will not worship that which you worship, nor are you worshipers of that which I worship." Remember, all these additional meanings are due to the inclusion of a single word that consists of two letters.

In the next verse we find a similar meaning at the start: instead of "I will not worship" it says "nor am I a worshiper of that which you worshiped." While it seems similar, and both speak of his rejection of the false deities, verse 2 speaks of his action, while verse 4 speaks of him, the person. Notice how he is referring to their actions in the past tense, then affirming that the prophet, peace be upon him, is not a worshiper of what they worshiped in the past. So now we have everything covered: past, present and future.

The next verse seems identical to verse 3. It looks like there is a repetition occurring, but this is not the case, for verse 3 is connected to the previous verse that speaks of the future, while verse 5 is connected to verse 4, which speaks about the past. Verse 5 says "and you are not worshipers of what I worship," but is referring to what he worshiped in the past, for this is connected to what they worshiped previously. However, the term "worship" is used to complete the repetition of verse 3, while also being clearer, since it removes any ambiguity that could suggest that he worshiped something else in the past. So in reality, while both verses have the exact same words, verse 3 is about the future, while verse 5 is about the past.

So let's summarize: Allah the Almighty is telling the prophet, peace be upon him, to tell them that he was never a worshiper of those that they worshiped in the past; he will never worship their idols in the future, for he is worshiping Allah alone. Even back in the past, before Islam, he was not a worshiper of their idols, so how could he start now, after becoming a messenger of the Almighty?

As for the end of the chapter, Allah orders the prophet, peace be upon him, to summarize the previous verses by saying to them: "To you is your religion, to me is my religion." It starts off with "to you", causing the sentence to mean: to you, and not to others, is this religion of yours; this religion is one that you came up with, hence it is yours. Also, the verse ends with "to me my religion" to keep with the flow of the verse, even though we understand from the context that it is referring to the religion of Allah. In other words, Allah is telling the prophet, peace be upon him, to tell the polytheists that their religion is theirs alone, and that his religion is that which he endorses, and it is what is practiced by the prophet, peace be upon him.

And there are more intricate things here. Look at how much is going on here. These two guys came thinking that there's nothing going on, it's just a bunch of repeated ramblings and words with no depth. Everything is covered, from past to present to future, intricate wordplay throughout, and all in just a few verses.

### FR182:20-144

Most of us are not aware of the eloquence within this verse. It seems pretty straightforward, doesn't seem like there's much to it. However, the word play is obvious to those that are familiar with Hebrew. The name Isaac, or Itzhak, literally means "he laughs". In other words, the wife laughed, and therefore she was blessed with a son named "he laughs". Then after Isaac, Jacob, or Yakov, which literally means "to follow": an intricate form of wordplay that is clearly intentional, with a clear understanding of the Hebrew language as well as a familiarity with Genesis 18:

"And he said, I will certainly return unto thee according to the time of life; and, lo, Sarah thy wife shall have a son. And Sarah heard it in the tent door, which was behind him. Now Abraham and Sarah were old and well stricken in age; and it ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women. Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?"

Interestingly, there's no mentioning of Jacob in the verses here, which proves that the word play isn't based on some sort of plagiarism, but it is due to knowledge of Hebrew.

How did classical first century Muslims understand these verses? We do find attributions to major classical exegetes like Mujahid, in which they state that [dahikat], meaning "laughed", actually meant "menstruated". In other words, Sarah menstruated and then was given the good news about her having Isaac. Now this understanding seems to have come from a classical line of poetry in which the word [dahikat] was used by an anonymous classical poet to mean "menstruated". However, others like Benue, who was from a Jewish background, took the verse literally, which is natural since he was familiar with Genesis. And Ibn [Qutayba], in the third century, points out that this literal understanding is supported by the Torah.

Interestingly, we do not find classical exegetes going into the meaning of Isaac or Jacob, apparently since they weren't aware of the significance of their names within the context of the verse. We do not find them refuting the biblical accounts either, or rejecting them.

What can we conclude from this? Well, we can conclude that the author of the Quran had access to information that classical exegetes did not have access to. The claim that Muhammad, peace be upon him, copied from the Bible conflicts with information that we would expect to find in classical works of tafsir. The claim that this information was available to society also is not in line with what we would expect to find in those works. And this is just one verse of many in which the Quran plays on words, makes puns and engages with scriptures in a different language, in a manner that wasn't even appreciated by the scholars of first century Arabia, with the exception of those from Jewish background that wholeheartedly accept Islam and ended up converting.
