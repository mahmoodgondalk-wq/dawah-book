### FR228:1-709
I've noticed some people on social media having no idea who [Albani] was, and I'm someone who is very influenced by [Albani] and very influenced by his revival of the hadith sciences. Hadith sciences were to a large degree [dead] until [Albani] influenced a generation of students of knowledge. Me and people my age, and of course those that are older than me, were introduced to [Albani] in a very natural way. Unfortunately, today a lot of people come across [Albani] through strange opinions, through condemnations, through some of the strange [views] that he held, which in reality do not do him justice.

In the year 1914 [Albani] was born in Albania, and his father was a Hanafi scholar. His father realized that secularism was spreading in Albania, and he wanted his children to have a good Islamic upbringing, and he didn't want his children to be influenced by secularism, so due to that he moved to Syria. [Albani] grew up in Syria; he moved there when he was nine years old. When he was there he didn't know a word of Arabic, but he was put into a school and he learned the language really fast. He learned Arabic so fast that his teacher used to criticize the other students for not learning it as fast as he did and not being as good at it as he was. The teacher would simply ask the students some questions in Arabic about grammar or whatnot, and they would fail to answer, and he would ask [Albani], who would get the answer correct.

[Albani] was later on taken away from school, and his father homeschooled him, taught him Quran, and taught him multiple Islamic sciences, but not hadith sciences. It was when he was around 19 years old that he came across criticisms of the hadith. [Albani] was surprised by this; it was the first time he came across something called hadith sciences, and he was quite influenced by it, found the copy of the book and started taking notes. He actually copied the full thing: he copied all the hadith, and then he copied all the criticisms and commentary. Then he realized there are many words in the hadith that he was not familiar with, so he got a dictionary or two, and he started to include the meanings of those words in the footnotes, and that of course helped [Albani] quite a lot. He gathered 2,000 pages of that, quite a large volume of work, at such a young age.

[Albani], from there, grew more attached to [ilm al-hadith], and grew so attached that he started to develop opinions that conflicted with his father's opinions, who was a staunch Hanafi. His father would stick to an opinion, then Albani himself would say: That conflicts with the hadith, and I'm not going to stick with that. That continued, and there weren't too many tensions at first, but with time the tensions grew.

At the time, believe it or not, in [Sham] you used to have two different prayers at the masjid: the Hanafi prayer and the [Shafi'i] prayer. Initially the Ahnaf used to pray first, and then after they're done with their prayer the Shafi's would pray. I know it's very strange, but that's how things were at the time. You cannot have a second [jama'ah]; you're supposed to pray the first jama'a, don't pray a second jamaat: this is an opinion that [al-Hasan al-Basri] holds, and he says that he's met 40 of the companions and they all prayed one [jama'ah]. So if you miss the jamaa, you don't pray another [jama'ah]; you pray by yourself.

Something strange happened at the time, which was: someone who was high up in the religious affairs changed things up, because he was a Shafi, so he wanted Shafi's to pray first. Due to that, things grew complicated, and al-Albani stopped praying with [the Ahnaf] and he started praying with the [Shafi'is], and his father was somewhat annoyed by that. But things got even worse when the imam of the masjid traveled, and Albani was left praying with the [Shafi'is] at first, while his father was the imam and leading the prayer with the [Hanafis]. People were talking: the son's not praying with the father, that's strange.

It got even worse when his father had to travel, and he told [Albani] to pray in his position and to lead the prayer, and [Albani] said: I'm sorry, but you know my opinion; I cannot do that. I'm going to pray in the first prayer; I will not pray in the second prayer. His father didn't want to have any more of that. The relationship went south really fast due to that, and he told him to come one day, and he sat him down and said to him: Listen, we cannot continue this. You're either going to do things the way I want you to do things, or you're going to have to leave. And [Albani] said: Give me three days, I'm going to think about it. He thought about it, and on the third day he told his father that he will leave.

His father gave him a very small amount of cash, something like 25 [liras], and that was it: [Albani] left home. Alhamdulillah, luckily he had some friends, students of knowledge, that helped him set himself up. What he did was he set up a shop for watch repairs; his father taught him how to repair watches when he was younger. What [Albani] really appreciated about watch repairs is you could get away with fixing some watches and making some cash from that and having free time for the rest of the day. So what [Albani] did was he focused on fixing watches three hours a day and spending the rest of his time seeking knowledge.

At that time Albani discovered [al-Zahiriyya], which is one of the greatest libraries in the world when it comes to preserving knowledge of hadith and hadith works. [Albani] used to visit extremely often, and at that time he used to spend many hours a day, sometimes six hours, sometimes eight hours, and there are reports that he used to spend 13 hours sometimes, to the extent that the employees used to lock him in, and they gave him a spare key for him to do his thing. He used to stand on the ladder sometimes (the books are high up), and he would climb up and he would spend an hour on the ladder going through some books. He took a table all for himself, and he used to put the manuscripts in front of him and take notes, and he spent his whole day doing that. It was a common thing; he'd just do this every single day, and it got somewhat annoying for the employees there, so they apparently cleaned out a storeroom and gave him an actual room in the library for him to do what he wanted to do.

He kept on doing that, and he kept on learning until he actually became an authority in [ilm al-hadith]. There's so much [in al-Zahiriyya] that no one had access to, and when I say no one had access to, I mean no one in the world [knew of], used or made use of. [Albani] took so many notes on what he came across that he was able to write volumes and volumes of works based on these manuscripts that were not even printed. I have with me twenty volumes of [weak] hadith and eleven volumes of authentic ahadith that [Albani] gathered, much of it based on the notes that he gathered during his time in [al-Zahiriyya].

It's quite remarkable when you see him doing the [takhrij] and him mentioning the sources that he relied upon. Today you'd have a much easier time because you're relying on a search engine; he didn't have a search engine. He would return to many sources; a lot of them were printed, but many of them were not printed. Many of them, again, no one had access to except for him, because he was the guy who was in [al-Zahiriyya] much of the day, and he just spent his time gathering all these sources.

[Albani] spent so much time reading that it affected his eyesight, and he once went to a doctor, and the doctor said: You need to seriously take some time off; you need to spend months, perhaps six months, without reading anything; it's messing with your eyesight. And [Albani] decided: Okay, I have no choice. So he stopped reading for a while. At the time he decided to try to make some use of his time by getting one of the manuscripts and having it transcribed by a scribe, and he got [Dhamm al-Malahi] by [Ibn Abi al-Dunya], and he gave it to the scribe, and he returned to him in a couple of weeks, if I'm not mistaken, and the scribe said: Okay, I'm done transcribing it; however, there's a page that's missing.

So [Albani] thought to himself: I need to find that one page; the whole thing is there, it's just one page that's missing. So he said: Maybe it's somewhere else in [al-Zahiriyya], I need to look; maybe it's put into another volume, because this book was included within a compilation of multiple manuscripts within a single volume. So he thought maybe it's within another one of the [majami']. So [Albani] went through all the [majami'] and started to take notes. He's like: I don't have to focus on reading too much; I'm just gonna go through it casually and take down notes about the works in the [Zahiriyya] while finding this final page. And he went through the whole thing, through majami, and he didn't find it. And then he went through all the books of hadith, and sooner or later he was done with [al-Zahiriyya] as a whole, and he had collected himself a catalogue of manuscripts. Imagine someone writing up a catalogue of manuscripts because he was looking for a single page of a manuscript! It's actually printed.

He became quite famous for being an authority in hadith, and he was later invited to teach at Medina, and he was there alongside major scholars like [Ibn Baz], [ash-Shinqiti]. He was very close to his students, and he was teaching them a science that no one knew anything about. He taught his students about [ilm al-isnad]; he taught people how to grade a hadith, how to weaken, how to strengthen a hadith. His students really loved him, and they used to gather around him, and he used to drop them off to home and to university in his car, and his car was always filled with students, and he's very friendly with them, and he used to play football with them as well. They used to say: You're wearing a [thobe], but you're playing football. And he's like: Yeah, it's good, it's sports, why not.

He was very close to students, and they benefited so much from him that it created some tension between him and some of the other professors at the university. For example, in one class the professor was teaching about a concept of the differences of opinions being a mercy upon the Muslims, and he quoted a very famous hadith which goes along the lines of: The differences of opinions among my nation is a mercy from Allah. So one of the students, he's like: We were told that this hadith is [a lie]. And he said: By whom? He said: Albani. So the sheikh actually went to [Albani], and he sat down and he asked him: How do you know it's [a lie]? And then [Albani] explained the whole matter. And awkwardly, the sheikh actually went back to his class and said: I sat down with [Albani], and [Albani] has no issues with the hadith; he said that the hadith is weak through one path but is authentic through multiple paths. [Albani] wasn't loved too much by some of the other professors there, because he actually created a revolution in the way the students looked at narrations.

It was something that I was quite influenced by. When I was at university I took an Islamic philosophy course (had nothing to do with my major, but I just was interested), and the professor one day mentioned the narration, or maybe one of the students mentioned a narration, I can't recall, and the narration was: If it were not for you, [O] Muhammad, I wouldn't have created the universe. So I raise my hand: Professor, this was mentioned, and this hadith is a fabrication. And he said to me: And who are you? Are you some sort of a muhaddith, for you to call this narration a fabrication? And I said: No, of course, I'm not a muhaddith, but this is what [Albani] said. And he said: Well, not everyone takes from [Albani]. And I said to him: Well then, I just shared that opinion for the students in the class that cared about [Albani]; for those that don't care, feel free to do whatever you like. He was quite upset, and my grades started to coincidentally stumble after that. Alhamdulillah, I didn't fail, of course.

Due to his tensions with some of the professors, his contract was not renewed, and he had to leave. He went to [Ibn Baz], and he was quite upset, and Ibn [Baz] told him: Don't worry; someone like you, wherever he goes, he will be of benefit (something along those lines). Unfortunately, when Albani returned to [Sham], there were some tensions between him and certain government officials, not because of positions that he held, not because of troublemaking or anything like that, but it was simply that [Albani] was hated by quite a lot of people from different sects, and they used to try to get him into trouble. When [Albani] got out of prison, he decided to go to Jordan, which is where he lived for the rest of his days. Again, when he actually moved to Jordan, there were some people that hated the sheikh, and they got him into trouble with the government, and he was actually taken to the border, and when he was at the border he was forced to actually leave the country. After being taken to the border, [Albani] just did not want to end up in Syria once again, so he actually escaped to Lebanon, and he spent the next couple of years jumping from country to country, from Lebanon to the Arab Emirates to Kuwait, until, alhamdulillah, Mohammed Ibrahim Shakra contacted the King of Jordan, who allowed [Albani] back in once again.
